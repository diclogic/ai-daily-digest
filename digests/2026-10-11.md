## Notable Papers & Research

### On the estimation and validity of AI time horizons — a statistical look at the METR plot
**Nguyen & Fithian** (UC Berkeley statistics, per secondary reporting) ([arXiv:2610.12466](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.12466), Oct 9)

The most consequential paper of the day, because it audits the chart the entire "AI progress is exponential" discourse rests on. METR's time-horizon metric — the length of task, measured in human completion time, that a model finishes with 50% probability — assumes task difficulty scales linearly with the log of human time. The authors drop that assumption, refitting **228 tasks across 26 models** with splines and item-response theory. The fitted curve is **nearly flat between 2 and 30 minutes** and roughly linear elsewhere, which means a jump from 3 minutes to 30 minutes is a much cheaper 10× than one from 30 minutes to 5 hours. Their point estimates also beat METR's under a cross-validated suite of proper scoring rules.

Read carefully: this is **not** a claim that the trend is fake. The paper's own framing is that time horizons should be interpreted alongside the construct-validity diagnostic plots it introduces, not quoted as a scalar. That is a methodological correction to a number that currently appears in investor decks and policy submissions. Authorship is from a secondary write-up ([DEV](https://dev.to/prabhakar_chaudhary_7afe4/is-a-10x-jump-always-a-10x-jump-a-statistical-audit-of-the-metr-time-horizon-plot-2g4b)) and we could not confirm it from the paper itself; METR has published its own [limitations note](https://metr.org/notes/2026-01-22-time-horizon-limitations/) separately, in January.

### BrickBench: Evaluating Agentic Brick Design
**Peter Kulits et al.** ([arXiv:2610.12452](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12452), submitted Oct 8)

300 prompts for physical assembly design across three regimes — **Model** (≤400 parts), **Set** (400–4,000 parts), and **Alt-Build** (fixed parts inventory) — shipped with **BrickAgent**, an environment giving agents construction tools plus interpretable validation of connectivity, collisions and stability. The headline number is a loss: human raters correctly picked the human-designed assembly in **323 of 360 comparisons**. The useful finding, per third-party analysis, is the gap between agents *with* the validation tools and agents without them ([Pebblous](https://blog.pebblous.ai/blog/brickbench-lego-design-benchmark/en/)) — a benchmark where tool access, not model scale, is the variable that moves the score.

### nanoMuse: an open-source personal agent for every device
**Guangyi Liu, Yong Liu, Jiangning Zhang** (Zhejiang University) ([arXiv:2610.08699](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08699), Oct 7; [GitHub](https://github.com/nano-muse/nanoMuse))

A cross-platform personal agent — Android, iOS, desktop, browser — that keeps working while the app is closed, holds persistent memory of its user, and **asks before any irreversible action**. It hit Hugging Face's trending papers on October 6 and tagged **v1.0.0 on October 9**. The consent-before-irreversible-action design is the part worth noting: it is the same control that this week's Anthropic unintended-actions report was effectively arguing for, arriving from an academic group rather than a frontier lab.

### Scott Aaronson: labs are testing their newest models against cryptographic primitives — "gingerly and discreetly"
([Andrew Curran relaying Aaronson](https://x.com/AndrewCurran_/status/2108034148791370002), Oct 9–10; primary source: Aaronson's Shtetl-Optimized)

The single most important statement of the day, and it is a disclosure rather than an opinion. Aaronson says his sources indicate **some AI companies are now using their latest internal models to attempt to break "important cryptographic protocols and primitives,"** doing so quietly. He arrived at it from an absence: OpenAI's October 6 release of 722 math manuscripts contained **strikingly little cryptography** relative to everything else it covered — which is what you would expect if the cryptographic results were the ones not being published.

Why it matters beyond the frisson: the release *did* include a claimed proof of the **Unique Games Conjecture** and of **L=BPL**, and UGC is the problem Aaronson's wife, **Dana Moshkovitz**, has worked on for her entire career. That is the personal register in which this arrived, and it is doing real argumentative work — the gap between "a lab published a proof of your life's problem" and "a lab is not publishing what it finds in your field" is the whole governance question in one researcher's inbox. Worth stating plainly: **no independently verified evidence** shows any lab has factored cryptographically significant integers, recovered real elliptic-curve keys, or defeated a deployed standard. This rests on unnamed sources and has no independent confirmation.

### Mathematicians organize against OpenAI — a professional body calls for a boycott
([the-decoder](https://the-decoder.com/some-mathematicians-call-for-openai-boycott-after-ai-generated-proofs-flood-their-field/), [Inside AI](https://insideai.news/news/ai-policy-and-regulation/openai-math-release-backlash/13860/), [Polymarket relay](https://x.com/Polymarket/status/2108014951340384504), Oct 9–10)

The **Association for Human Mathematics** — a US body reported at **752 members**, whose members pledge not to work with commercial AI companies or publish AI-generated mathematical writing — urged mathematicians to **"discontinue their work with OpenAI."** Its stated grounds are procedural: it says the Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence, which OpenAI cites, had **advised against testing advanced math problems on internal models**. The statement names no manuscript, alleges no incorrect proof, and specifies no sanctions. OpenAI has not responded publicly. Reaction is split, with economist **Noah Smith** among those arguing mathematicians do not own the field.

The sequence matters: OpenAI released **722 manuscripts covering 372 result groups** on October 6, pulled **three** the next day over a sign error (a `+1` where its own conventions required `-1`, which flipped a count that should have cancelled to zero), revised **14**, and ended at 719. OpenAI's position is that the retraction applies to the proofs, not the propositions — which, as critics noted, returns an unrefuted claim with a broken proof to the status of conjecture. Only about **42% of top-line results have Lean proofs** and outsiders cannot rerun the model. Reported solve rate: **~5% of roughly 8,000 attempted open problems** at about 3 hours of compute each, though counts vary by source. This is the first organized professional-body refusal of a frontier lab's output, and it is about norms rather than accuracy.

**1. [rtk-ai/rtk](https://github.com/rtk-ai/rtk)** — **~82.8–82.9k stars**, Rust
A single-binary CLI proxy that intercepts bash output before an agent reads it, advertising a **60–90% cut in token consumption** on common dev commands. Zero dependencies, 175 contributors. **Why it's here:** not velocity — it is adding only ~**628 stars/week** at this size — but because it is the first widely-adopted efficiency tool to get an independent counter-benchmark. [Quesma](https://quesma.com/blog/does-rtk-make-ai-coding-cheaper/) argues the savings don't translate to lower bills and cites a JetBrains SkillsBench run that found **no savings at all**. To its credit the README is more careful than the tagline: it says RTK cuts up to 90% of the bash output the agent *reads*, which "is not the same as cutting your bill by 90%," and that its reported token counts are estimated as **bytes/4**. Real scope limit: the auto-rewrite hook only covers **Bash** calls — Read, Grep and Glob bypass it entirely. A useful case study in how a 82k-star project's claim survives contact with a benchmark.

**2. [franzenzenhofer/big-arrow-on-the-screen](https://github.com/franzenzenhofer/big-arrow-on-the-screen)** — **~379–412 points on HN, 165–188 comments** ([thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50018817))
Lets an agent paint large arrows, boxes and text directly onto your screen, so you can see where it thinks it is pointing. **Why it's trending:** it is the day's top AI item on Hacker News, and it is a debugging tool disguised as a gimmick — it makes agent intent legible in real time, which is the same problem the METR audit and the deception-probe papers are attacking from the measurement side. HN's reaction is split: enthusiasm for the visual feedback, with a substantial thread questioning the security implications of giving an agent an unrestricted screen overlay. Point counts differ across digest snapshots.

**3. [rociiu/talorys](https://github.com/rociiu/talorys)** — **~246 points on HN**, TypeScript, MIT
A single-user personal AI agent — chat, editable memory, tasks, notes, scheduled reminders and daily digests — that deploys entirely into **your own Cloudflare account** with `npx create-talorys@latest`. The architecture is the interesting part: a Pages site and `/api` Function forward over a **service binding to a private Worker with no public URL**, which routes to a Durable Object with SQLite storage, using Workers AI for chat and **Durable Object alarms** for automations. **Why it's trending:** free-tier friendly, no telemetry, and the maintainers operate no server or database of their own — the self-hosted answer to the personal-agent category that nanoMuse is approaching from academia. The author calls it early and is asking for feedback; forks are already circulating (`KrunchMuffin/talorys`, `who/talorys-semantic`).

**4. [manaflow-ai/cmux](https://github.com/manaflow-ai/cmux)** — **~28.1k stars, ~2.5k forks**
A Ghostty-based macOS terminal built around supervising multiple coding agents: vertical tabs, a **notification ring on any pane whose agent needs attention**, a sidebar showing git branch, PR status and listening ports, a scriptable in-app browser, and an `ssh` command that spins up a remote workspace. **Why it's trending:** it is the clearest instance of a new tool category — not an agent, but a cockpit for running several at once — and the org ships the supporting pieces separately (`cmux-skills` for Claude Code and Codex, `cmux-home` as a launcher TUI, a Homebrew tap). The constraint to note: macOS only.
