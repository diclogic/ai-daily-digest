## Notable Papers & Research

### Language models tell you your new idea has already been done

**"Language Models Make New Research Sound Old"** — Guy Zyskind, [arXiv:2611.23546](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23546) (posted **November 17–18**).

The finding is uncomfortable and specific. On **40 research questions drawn from newly accepted papers** — questions that by construction had not been studied — **GPT-6-Astra correctly judges 98% of them to be unstudied when asked directly**, but **states that status plainly in only 8% of its ordinary answers**. Asked instead to *grade* novelty against **RINoBench** (research ideas from ICLR 2022 and 2023 submissions paired with reviewers' 1–5 novelty grades), the models' grades **barely separate studied from open questions** even though their direct judgements separate them well.

*Why it matters:* this is the mechanism behind a complaint many researchers have voiced anecdotally — that asking a frontier model about your idea returns a confident wall of adjacent prior work. The paper says the knowledge is there and the framing suppresses it. The authors also raise the possibility that models **already know the paper they are being asked to judge**, which would make novelty grading unmeasurable by this route. *Caveat:* only the abstract was readable from this session; the 98%/8% split is the paper's own claim and the benchmark is small (40 questions).

### RoboBuilder: coding agents that design robots, and 135 of them actually got built

**"RoboBuilder: Agentic Robot Co-Design with Real-world Video Feedback in the Loop"** — Ghadi Nehme et al., [arXiv:2611.23855](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23855), cs.AI (posted **November 18**).

A benchmark where coding agents design **both the hardware and the control program** for a robot from standard parts, motors and a single custom 3D-printed piece — and every design that passes in simulation is **physically built and run on a real obstacle course**. Reported scale: **16,427 simulations of 12,774 distinct robots, 135 physical robots built, an estimated 240 hours of build time**.

Two numbers carry the paper. **66% of simulated passes hold up in the real world** across all levels — the sim-to-real gap quantified rather than asserted. And when each real failure is returned to the agent **as video**, so the agent can redesign and also *correct its own simulator* where the video shows a modelling error, **72% of level-1 and level-2 failures are eliminated**. Agents handle nearly all easy obstacles and still struggle badly on the harder ones.

*Why it matters:* almost every agentic-design result this year stops at simulation. This one pays the cost of building the thing, and the headline result is that a third of what looks right in sim is wrong on the floor.

### A software-reliability argument against model-level reliability claims

**"Reliability is a user-specific property of large language models"** — Xingyu Zhao, [arXiv:2611.23732](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23732), cs.SE (posted **November 18**, single author).

A position paper arguing that LLM reliability is **not a fixed property of a model** and cannot be read off a leaderboard: it is a failure rate relative to a particular **operational profile** — a specific user, their inputs, their usage conditions. Borrowing from classical software reliability engineering, it separates **testing that hunts for faults** from **testing that estimates how often failures will occur in future use**, lists ten modelling considerations behind any reliability claim, and proposes a roadmap over four linked streams: **operational profile, oracle error, local probabilistic robustness, and dependence**. It explicitly treats reliability claims as *complementary to*, not a substitute for, separate safety and security arguments.

*Why it matters:* it is the clean theoretical statement of what the GPT-6.2 reception demonstrated empirically this week — a model can gain on every published benchmark and get worse for a given developer's actual workload, and both facts can be true at once.

**What is left is a theme rather than a pile of stars: three of today's four are gateways and harnesses for subscriptions and agents you already pay for. That is where the open-source energy has gone.**

**1. [Wei-Shaw/sub2api](https://github.com/Wei-Shaw/sub2api)** — *"AI API Gateway Platform for Subscription Quota Distribution."* **LGPL-3.0**, **46.3k stars, +92 today**, **10.0k forks** — a **22% fork ratio** — **8,224 commits** on `main`, **2.8k open issues**, **1.1k open PRs**. It issues platform API keys to users and forwards their requests to upstream **Claude, OpenAI, Gemini and Grok/xAI** accounts, handling authentication, billing, load balancing and routing in between: multi-account OAuth and API-key management, token-level cost accounting, sticky sessions, per-user and per-account concurrency caps, composite groups for cross-provider routing, a Simple Mode for individuals, and **built-in payments via EasyPay, Alipay, WeChat Pay and Stripe**. *Why it's here:* it is the distribution layer for subscription quota — the same shape as yesterday's **Agent2API**, but with a payment system attached, which makes its purpose explicit. *Caveats, and they are the point:* the README itself says use **"may violate the terms of service of Anthropic and other upstream providers,"** that **users bear that risk**, that the project is **for technical learning and research**, and that the authors **disclaim liability for account bans, outages and data loss** and have **authorized no commercial operation** based on it. Also stated: **URL validation can be disabled and HTTP is allowed by default in dev-friendly mode** (explicitly "not suitable for production"); an existing `config.yaml` **skips the setup wizard and can leave no admin account**; image upstreams may return a different format than requested and **retries incur extra charges**; Simple Mode's spending window is a **post-request soft cap** that concurrent requests can overshoot. The **2.8k open issues against 8,224 commits** is the heaviest queue in this section's recent history, and the README carries **extensive third-party sponsor and affiliate advertising** for competing relay services.

**2. [QuantumNous/new-api](https://github.com/QuantumNous/new-api)** — *"An AI gateway for models, applications, and agents."* **AGPL-3.0 with additional Section 7 terms**, **52.2k stars, +80 today**, **12.7k forks**, **6,713 commits**, **785 issues** and **698 PRs** listed. The legitimate-deployment sibling of the above, and architecturally more serious: one consistent API in front of **OpenAI Chat Completions and Responses, Anthropic Messages and Gemini**, with upstreams including **Azure OpenAI, AWS Bedrock, Vertex AI, DeepSeek and Qwen**. Model mappings, channel priorities and weights, retries, channel affinity, quotas and subscriptions, **expression-based pricing by usage tier**, cache accounting, and real access control — fine-grained permissions, **OAuth/OIDC, passkeys, 2FA**, session management. Async image and video task APIs extend via **JavaScript plugins**; the console ships in **seven languages**; Docker with SQLite for a quick start, Compose with PostgreSQL and Redis for real use, plus MySQL and ClickHouse. Built on **One API** (MIT). *Why it's trending:* the fork count — **12.7k** — says the multi-provider gateway has become default infrastructure rather than a workaround. *Caveats, from the README:* protocol conversion between the four text APIs **depends on channel and model, and some tools and fields do not map exactly**; the **`latest` Docker tag moves**, so versions should be pinned; **multi-node deployments must share the main database and auth secrets**, and separate Redis instances **count rate limits independently per node**; screenshots use **simulated data**. Notably, the README states up front that operating a public generative-AI or API-resale service may require **filings, licensing, content safety measures, real-name verification, log retention, tax compliance and upstream authorization** — and says to complete those first. The **AGPL Section 7 terms** require keeping attribution and, for modified versions with a UI, a visible link back to the project.

**3. [vercel/eve](https://github.com/vercel/eve)** — *"A filesystem-first framework for durable AI agents."* **Apache-2.0**, **6.0k stars, +42 today**, **665 forks**, **2,683 commits**, **423 open issues**, **529 open PRs**. The premise is a convention rather than an abstraction: an agent is a **directory**. `agent/instructions.md` is the required system prompt, `agent.ts` is optional model and runtime config, and optional folders hold **tools, skills, channels (HTTP, Slack, Discord) and cron schedules** — so an agent can be read, diffed and reviewed as files instead of inspected through a framework's object graph. Tools are plain **TypeScript functions with input schemas** (Zod in the README's example). `npx eve@latest init my-agent` scaffolds, installs, inits Git and opens a terminal UI; `--model` selects any AI Gateway model and `--reasoning` sets effort; the default is **`openai/gpt-6-luna-fast`**. Web Chat is two commands (`npx eve link`, `npx eve add channel/web`) and `npx eve deploy` publishes. Full docs ship **inside the npm package** at `node_modules/eve/docs`. *Why it's here:* it is the most direct answer yet to the thing that makes agent frameworks unmaintainable — that the agent's actual behaviour is scattered across code, config and prompt strings. Filesystem-first makes the prompt a reviewable artifact. *Caveats:* the README states **no limitations section**, which is itself worth noting at 6k stars; **529 open PRs against 2,683 commits** is an unusual ratio and suggests contributions are arriving faster than they are merged; Web Chat **requires linking a Vercel project and Vercel sign-in** to provision team authentication, though local development does not; `--model` without `--reasoning` silently uses the provider default; and the sample weather tool **returns mock data**, so the quick start is a demo, not a working integration.

**4. [Dodaios/Dodaios](https://github.com/Dodaios/Dodaios)** — *"Your chief of staff for coding agents."* **Apache-2.0** (with NOTICE attribution and a separate trademark policy for the name and logo), **426 stars, +19 today** — **~4.5%, the highest relative daily velocity of any new AI repo on today's pages** — **147 forks**, **6,668 commits**, **22 open issues**, **12 open PRs**. Formerly **Personal Jarvis**. You describe a task by **voice or chat** and its assistant writes the brief, launches the right agent — **Claude Code, Codex** — inside a built-in **Agentic IDE**, monitors the run, and tells you when it is done or stuck. It runs several agents side by side and tracks what each one did, works off **Claude or ChatGPT subscriptions, API keys for Claude/OpenAI/Gemini, or local Ollama**, and ships desktop installers for Windows, macOS and Linux plus a **pipx server package and headless mode** for machines with no screen. Plugins cover Google, Slack and Figma, and there is a phone-call option. *Why it's here:* **147 forks against 426 stars (34%)** and **6,668 commits** on a repo this small is the profile of a project people are running, not starring — and the orchestration-above-agents layer is exactly where the multi-CLI era is short. *Caveats, all from the README:* **installers are unsigned** (Windows warns; macOS needs a one-time System Settings allow); **only Windows 11 has a recorded live sign-off**; the **default permission mode is "Bypass permissions," where nothing asks for confirmation** — users must opt into an asking mode, with only four hard limits always requiring approval; most plugins carry a **Preview** badge; **phone calls need your own Twilio number** and are an early preview; **sign-in for Google, Slack and Figma routes through a token service the project runs**; the terminal installer needs **Python 3.11–3.14 and Git**; Dodaios is free but **each coding agent still needs its own paid plan or key**; and the project is **independent and not endorsed** by the makers of the tools it drives. That default bypass mode is the one to read twice before pointing this at a real repository.
